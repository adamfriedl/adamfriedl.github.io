justice-gap-map

November 2026 – ongoing

Where are debt-collection lawsuits filed, and how does that line up with the civil legal aid available? Debt collectors file hundreds of thousands of suits a year in state courts, and many end in default judgments. This project pulls public court, credit, Census, and legal aid data into county tables and an interactive map. So far it covers the three states whose courts publish debt suits by county in a form a script can read: Texas, Indiana, and Virginia.

It's descriptive. It reports what public records show about places, not why, and it doesn't judge legal aid providers or their funders. There's no national count of debt lawsuits, so a blank state on the map means no data, not no lawsuits.

  • I checked the obvious measures before building on them, and two failed. CFPB complaints don't measure need: in Texas, the poorest counties have the most debt in collections and the fewest complaints. Federal legal aid funding doesn't measure capacity: the formula is nearly flat per eligible person. Court filings and credit data replaced them.
  • Texas justice courts took 106,061 debt claims in 2014 and 518,852 in 2025. The highest rates are in fast-growing suburbs around Dallas–Fort Worth and Houston, not the poorer border counties, so no single national measure stands in for court data.
  • The pipeline is Python and DuckDB, moving onto AWS with all infrastructure in Terraform: GitHub Actions signs in with short-lived tokens, posts a plan on every pull request, and applies on merge. I build it with AI coding agents under written rules, including read-only cloud access for agents and no names or addresses of people sued, ever.

pad-lab

July – September 2026

A small data platform lab built on real Federal Election Commission campaign-finance data. Python loaders pull from the FEC's public API into a Cloud Storage landing bucket, then BigQuery, where dbt models clean the data and build the tables behind a small public dashboard. It runs once a day on its own.

It's modeled on Community Tech Alliance's Progressive Action Database (PAD), though it isn't affiliated with CTA. I built it to learn PAD-style data platform architecture hands-on. I wanted to walk each layer myself and work through observability, cost, and data quality on a real dataset.

  • I wrote all the infrastructure in Terraform, including the scheduled job, alerts for a failed run or stale data, and a billing budget.
  • Loads are incremental, picking up from the last date seen with a seven-day overlap, and dbt tests on the data fail the job, so a bad load trips the same alert as a crash.
  • I learned the cost side of the choices: a managed Airflow environment costs hundreds a month just sitting idle, so a scheduled serverless job taught me the same scheduling and alerting patterns for a dollar or two.

intake-classification-eval

October – November 2026

Two runs of a model against the "User Intake Classification" dataset from the Stanford Legal Design Lab's Legal Help Commons. The dataset is thoughtfully built. Alongside ordinary cases it includes deliberate tests: messages with crisis words that aren't emergencies, messages with no legal issue at all, people caught between two jurisdictions, hostile or disorganized writers. That makes it a far better test of a legal aid intake tool than a plain list of cases, and it's why I wanted to work with it.

  • The biggest lesson was that the prompt is part of what gets measured. I ran the same model twice, changing only the instructions. The second prompt follows the key's labeling conventions (one code at the routing level of the taxonomy, the county where the person lives), and exact issue-code matches went from 9% to 23%.
  • On emergencies, the two prompts erred in opposite directions. The first ("within days") missed 41 of the key's 92 emergencies. The second, which counts any open deadline or scheduled hearing, missed 4 but raised 27 false alarms. Where that line belongs is a legal judgment, not something to tune for.

Both runs caught all 10 of the dataset's designed crisis scenarios. The second prompt was written after studying the key and the first run's errors, so its scores are optimistic; the useful result is the gap between the runs. One run per prompt, 180 synthetic messages. The scripts were written with Claude Code, and I reviewed them and the scoring choices; a second, independently written scorer checked the figures.

homelab

May – September 2026

A personal Google Cloud lab. I wrote the Terraform for a small environment as reusable modules (a network with Cloud NAT, a VM, BigQuery, IAM), with SSH only through IAP and OS Login and no open SSH from the Internet.

At work I've run and operated Terraform; here I wrote it and the CI around it from scratch. The CI uses no stored keys: GitHub Actions authenticates through Workload Identity Federation, runs terraform plan on every pull request, applies on merge to main, then configures the VM with Ansible.
